EU AI ACT GUIDE

EU AI Act Requirements Checklist (2026)

Everything your company must do under the EU AI Act — who it applies to, what's in force right now, and the fines for getting it wrong.

Last updated: November 13, 2026 · ~8 min read
The law is live. Enforcement of the EU AI Act's core transparency obligations started August 2, 2026. Authorities can now investigate, impose fines, and order AI products off the market. The checklist below tells you exactly where you stand.

1. Who must comply

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) applies far beyond the EU. It catches any company that supplies, deploys, imports, or represents AI systems used by people in the EU — regardless of where the company is based.

  • Providers — companies that develop or place an AI system on the EU market.
  • Deployers — companies that use an AI system in the course of their activity (even a chatbot built on OpenAI or Anthropic APIs).
  • Importers & distributors — businesses that bring AI systems into the EU or resell them.
  • Authorised representatives — certain non-EU providers must appoint an EU authorised representative where the Act requires it; this is not a blanket rule for every business with EU customers.

A company established outside the EU is not automatically covered simply because its website is accessible in Europe. The Act can cover providers placing systems on the EU market or putting them into service, and providers or deployers where the system’s output is used in the EU. Assess your product, customers, role and use case rather than relying on company location alone.

Note on the 2026 Omnibus: Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 sets the application dates for high-risk requirements at December 2, 2027 for Annex III and August 2, 2028 for Annex I. The general application date remains August 2, 2026, while specific provisions and transitional arrangements have their own dates. Certain new Article 5 prohibitions and the Article 50(2) transition for some systems already on the market apply from December 2, 2026. SMEs and start-ups are not automatically exempt; Article 99 requires their interests and economic viability to be considered when penalties are imposed.

3. Classify your system

Your obligations depend on the risk tier your AI system falls into. This is the single most important classification step.

  • Unacceptable risk — prohibited: social scoring by public authorities, subliminal manipulation, exploitation of vulnerable groups, untargeted facial scraping. Banning already in effect.
  • High risk — Annex I (products under EU safety law) and Annex III (critical infrastructure, education, employment, credit, essential services, law enforcement, migration, justice, democracy). Subject to the heaviest requirements.
  • GPAI / systemic risk — general-purpose models, with extra duties if they reach high impact capability (10^25 FLOPs or more).
  • Limited risk — transparency obligations (chatbots, deepfakes, AI-generated content).
  • Minimal risk — the vast majority of internal tools; encouraged to follow voluntary codes.
Common mistake: companies assume their internal RAG chatbot is "minimal risk." If it touches employment decisions, credit scoring, or student assessment, it is high risk — with a much heavier compliance burden.

4. Transparency obligations (in force now)

These are the live obligations since August 2, 2026. If you operate any AI that interacts with people, you must check these today:

AI interaction disclosure (Article 50(1)). Providers must design systems intended to interact directly with people so that people are informed they are interacting with AI, unless this is obvious to a reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect person in the circumstances.
Machine-readable marking (Article 50(2)). Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, image, video or text must ensure outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated, subject to the Act’s technical feasibility and other conditions.
Deepfake and public-interest text disclosure (Article 50(4)). Deployers using AI to generate or manipulate a deepfake must disclose that it was artificially generated or manipulated. Deployers publishing AI-generated or manipulated text on matters of public interest must disclose this too, unless a relevant human review/editorial-control exception applies.
Emotion recognition and biometric categorisation (Article 50(3)). Deployers must inform exposed individuals when they use emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation systems, subject to the Act’s specific exceptions.

Article 50 does not create one generic AI-label rule: it assigns different duties to providers and deployers. Map each feature to the correct paragraph, identify who controls the system and output, and check the transition for systems already placed on the market before 2 August 2026.

5. High-risk AI requirements (Articles 8–15)

The high-risk requirements in Chapter III, Sections 1–3 apply from December 2, 2027 for Annex III systems and August 2, 2028 for Annex I product-related systems, subject to the amended Article 113 mechanism and any earlier date resulting from a Commission decision confirming that support measures are available. Even where deadlines moved, the smartest teams are building now — remediation takes months, and the proverbial "black box" audit is the classic failure mode.

Risk management system (Art. 9). Continuous risk identification, analysis, and mitigation throughout the lifecycle.
Data & data governance (Art. 10). Relevant, representative, error-free training/validation data; bias-compliant design; documented lineage.
Technical documentation (Art. 11). Full documentation proving compliance — available for authorities on request.
Record-keeping & logging (Art. 12). Automatic event logs covering the system's full lifecycle.
Human oversight (Art. 14). Measures that let humans supervise, interpret, and override the system's output.
Accuracy, robustness & cybersecurity (Art. 15). Documented performance metrics, resilience to errors and adversarial attacks.
Conformity assessment (Art. 43) + EU Declaration (Art. 47). Self-assessment or notified-body assessment depending on the use case; CE marking.
Registration in the EU database (Art. 49). High-risk systems must be registered before market placement.
Post-market monitoring (Art. 72) and serious-incident reporting to national authorities.

6. General-purpose AI (GPAI) obligations

In force since August 2, 2025 for model providers:

  • Provide detailed technical documentation and basic information to downstream providers.
  • Respect the EU Copyright Directive — including a copyright policy (this is the rule behind major legal disputes over training data).
  • Publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content.
  • Systemic-risk models: the Act provides a presumption of high-impact capabilities where cumulative compute used for training exceeds 1025 FLOPs, while also allowing designation through applicable criteria. Additional evaluation, adversarial testing, incident reporting and cybersecurity duties apply. Do not treat the compute figure as the only route to systemic-risk classification.

7. Governance, enforcement & fines

  • Each EU country designates a market surveillance authority to investigate and fine companies.
  • The Brussels-based AI Office oversees GPAI models and systemic-risk enforcement across the EU.
  • National competent authorities and market-surveillance authorities supervise compliance and can use corrective measures under the Act, including restricting, withdrawing or recalling non-compliant systems where legal conditions are met.
  • Fines are tiered:
    • €35M or 7% of global annual revenue (whichever is higher) — for prohibited practices.
    • €15M or 3% — for most other violations (e.g. high-risk obligations).
    • €7.5M or 1.5% — for supplying incorrect information to authorities.

8. Your 7-step action plan

  1. Inventory your AI — list every model, chatbot, generator, and automation you ship or use.
  2. Classify each system into unacceptable / high / GPAI / limited / minimal risk.
  3. Fix transparency now — label chatbots and AI-generated content, add watermarking. These are live obligations.
  4. Document everything — model cards, data lineage, oversight design, and logging.
  5. Close high-risk gaps (Articles 9–15) ahead of the applicable Annex III or Annex I date, and monitor Commission decisions under amended Article 113.
  6. Produce the technical file — EU declaration of conformity + conformity report.
  7. Monitor continuously — the rules are moving fast; treat compliance as a process, not a project.

Frequently asked questions

Does the EU AI Act apply to companies outside the EU?

It can. The Act’s territorial scope includes certain providers placing AI systems on the EU market or putting them into service, and providers or deployers where the system’s output is used in the EU. Being outside the EU does not by itself create an exemption, but EU website accessibility alone does not automatically settle the question.

Are all high-risk AI requirements already enforceable in 2026?

No. The general application date is August 2, 2026, but the amended Article 113 sets December 2, 2027 for most Annex III high-risk systems and August 2, 2028 for Annex I product-related systems. Check the system category, transition rules and any applicable exceptions.

What should an AI chatbot do under Article 50?

Providers must design systems intended to interact directly with people so that people are informed they are interacting with AI, unless that is obvious in context. Other Article 50 duties cover synthetic-output marking, deepfake disclosure and certain public-interest text disclosures; these duties are assigned differently to providers and deployers.

Do small businesses have a blanket exemption?

No. SMEs and start-ups do not receive a blanket exemption from the AI Act. Some obligations and support measures are tailored to smaller organisations, and the penalty framework requires proportionality considerations, but you must still assess the rules that apply to your role and system.

How can I start checking an AI system?

Start by inventorying the system, documenting its intended purpose and users, identifying whether you are a provider or deployer, and assessing the risk category.
